# Sedimentary Order

*A design philosophy for systems that only ever accumulate.*

Sedimentary Order holds that structure is not imposed on a surface but
deposited onto it, mark by mark, until meaning settles out of repetition.
The canvas is treated as a cross-section through time: thin horizontal strata
laid one above another, none ever erased, each slightly different in weight
and spacing, so that the eye reads history the way a geologist reads a cliff.
Every composition should feel meticulously crafted — the product of patient,
painstaking attention, as if each hairline were drawn by hand over many hours
by someone who has spent a lifetime learning where a line should stop.

Space is governed by a single axis of accumulation. One vertical spine carries
the densest marks; everything else orbits it at measured distances, held in
place by fine tensioned lines rather than boxes. Forms are perfect and few —
circles, hairlines, small squares — and they are allowed to breathe within
generous negative space. Nothing touches unless it means to. Balance comes
from weight, not symmetry: a dense column on one side answered by an
emptiness of exactly the right size on the other. Master-level execution here
means every gap is deliberate and every alignment has been refined repeatedly.

Colour is mineral and restrained: an unbleached paper ground, a warm
near-black ink, and a single ochre — the colour of a seed husk or of old
varnish — used sparingly to mark the moments that matter: a commitment, a
seal, a chosen one among many. Greys are not separate colours but ink at
lower pressure. The palette should feel calibrated with the care of a
printmaker pulling a proof, never decorative, never loud.

Scale works in two registers. At a distance the piece reads as one quiet
architectural figure; up close it rewards scrutiny with dense fields of
repeated marks, tiny reference codes, numbered strata, and diagrams within
diagrams — the visual language of a field notebook from a discipline that does
not exist. Rhythm comes from repetition with small variance: the same mark,
again and again, each instance slightly unique, so the whole has the pulse of
something grown rather than generated.

Typography is whispered. Thin, widely tracked capitals name things; a
monospaced hand annotates them in the smallest legible size; a single serif
italic, used once, carries the only sentence. Words never explain — they
anchor what the forms already show, like labels pinned beside specimens.
The finished work must look like an artefact of deep expertise: a plate
from a scientific atlas, labored over with reverence, where the invisible
has been patiently measured into view.
